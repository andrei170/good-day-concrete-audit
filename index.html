Three things on your site that are not yours

One: the offer is written for a car wash

Your homepage carries this promotion:

gooddayconcrete.net · homepage Big Savings: 10% OFF Any Detail Service of $99 and Up

"Detail service" is auto-detailing language. Concrete contractors do not sell detail services. This is template copy written for a different trade that was never replaced.

It is also the wrong offer regardless. A ten percent discount on a ninety-nine dollar job is ten dollars. Your actual work is driveways, foundations and patios worth thousands. The one promotional message on your site is aimed at a customer you do not want.

Two: two different founding dates

Your site displays "Since 2010" and "25 Years of Experience" as badges, next to each other. 2010 was sixteen years ago.

Both can be true if you personally have twenty-five years in concrete and founded the company in 2010, but the site does not say that, so a customer reads it as a business that cannot keep its own story straight. Pick one and explain it.

Three: the gallery cannot be verified as your work

Every photograph in your gallery is named sequentially — gallery1.jpg through gallery19.jpg — and stored in the shared template folder rather than uploaded individually. That is the naming pattern of images supplied with the template.

We cannot tell from the outside whether these are your pours or stock supplied by the platform. A customer cannot tell either, and that is the problem. If they are your jobs, they need captions, locations and dates. If they are not, they need replacing with photos from your phone.

On the CRM question you asked

The honest sequence

A CRM is worth having, but it solves the problem after the enquiry arrives — chasing quotes, following up, stopping jobs falling through the cracks. It does not create enquiries.

Right now your form asks nothing, so a CRM would be catching leads that contain a name and a sentence. Fix the form first and the CRM has something worth managing. Ask for job type, approximate square footage, the address and the timeline, and every enquiry arrives already priceable.

Then the CRM does the part you actually want — automatic reply within seconds, follow-up if you have not quoted in two days, and a record of every job you quoted and lost so you can go back to them next season.

The good news is you already have the measurement layer installed, which is the piece most contractors are missing and the piece that makes everything else provable.

Estimated size of the business

Estimate

Revenue roughly $400,000 to $900,000. Net profit roughly $50,000 to $130,000.

How we got there. You run residential, commercial and city work with your own excavation capability, which means equipment and a crew rather than a one-man operation. Denver residential concrete typically prices around $8 to $18 per square foot for flatwork, putting a standard driveway between roughly $6,000 and $15,000 and a patio between $4,000 and $10,000. A crew completing three to six jobs a month across a Denver season, plus city contract work, lands in that band.

Concrete contractors typically run 10 to 15 percent net once material, equipment and labour are covered.

The relevant part for the CRM conversation. At that revenue, the difference between quoting eight jobs and winning three versus winning five is worth tens of thousands a year, and it is entirely a follow-up problem rather than a lead problem. That is precisely what a CRM fixes, and it is why the form has to ask better questions first.

Priority order

  1. Remove the "detail service" offer

    It is another trade's copy and it advertises a discount on jobs you do not want. Replace it with something aimed at driveways and patios, or remove it entirely.

  2. Put your Google rating and review count on the site

    You reference Google Reviews but never show the number. In Denver home services this is the figure customers compare on.

  3. Rebuild the form to qualify

    Job type, square footage, address, timeline. This is the step that makes a CRM worth buying and cuts the time you spend on jobs you were never going to win.

  4. Cut twenty-two services down to five

    Lead with the work you want more of. The rest can live on a secondary page. Twenty-two keyword-stuffed services helps nobody, including Google.

  5. Fix the founding date and get a business email

    Pick one story about your experience and tell it properly, and move off Gmail onto your own domain.

  6. Caption the gallery or replace it

    Location, job type and date on every photo. If any images came with the template, swap them for your own pours.
